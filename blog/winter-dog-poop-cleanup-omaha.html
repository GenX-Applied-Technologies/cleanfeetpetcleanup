Scooping Dog Poop in Winter

Snow hides the mess. Here's why it still matters, and how to skip the spring thaw surprise.

Omaha winters have a way of making the backyard look perfectly clean. A fresh layer of snow covers everything, the cold keeps the smell down, and it feels easy to wait until spring to deal with it. But your dog doesn't take the winter off, and neither does what they leave behind. Every pile that gets buried in December is still sitting there in March.

What Happens to Dog Poop Under the Snow

Cold weather doesn't break dog waste down. It mostly just puts it on pause. The bigger concern is what's inside it. A study published in the journal Parasites & Vectors notes that whipworm eggs from dog waste "survive from cold winter to hot summer, especially in wet and shady areas," and that parasite eggs left in the environment can stay infectious for years.

Roundworms are another common one. According to Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center, roundworm eggs passed in a dog's stool become infectious to other dogs 2 to 4 weeks later, and people can be infected too if they accidentally swallow an egg from contaminated soil. Cornell's first prevention tip is simple: prompt removal and disposal of feces. A freezing yard doesn't change that advice.

The Spring Thaw Problem

If you've lived in Omaha for a while, you've seen it. The snow melts in March and the whole winter shows up at once. Piles that would have taken a few seconds each to pick up in January are now soggy, smeared into the grass, and harder to get off the lawn. It's also the time of year kids and dogs are most excited to be back outside.

Melting snow and spring rain also move things around. The City's stormwater program, Omaha Stormwater, lists "not picking up pet waste" as one of the common ways people contribute to stormwater pollution, and asks pet owners to pick up after their pets and put the waste in the garbage.

Tips for Scooping in Winter

If you're handling it yourself this winter, a few habits make it a lot easier:

  • Shovel a potty spot. Clear a small area close to the door. Most dogs will happily use it instead of wading into deep snow, which keeps everything in one place.
  • Scoop when it's frozen. Frozen piles lift off the snow in one piece. A quick pass on a cold morning is much cleaner than waiting for a warm afternoon.
  • Pick up before the next snowfall. Once a pile gets buried, you won't find it again until the thaw.
  • Bag it and trash it. Keep a lidded can near the door so you're not walking bags across the yard. Don't leave it on the lawn or toss it near a storm drain.
  • Clean up after yourself. Wash your hands and knock the snow off your boots before you head back inside.
